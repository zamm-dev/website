Last year I went to [a meditation retreat](/blog/digestibles/v1.1.0/presentness/) where I properly learned about presentness for the first time. It was an expensive retreat, but you definitely got what you were paying for and much more. Whether it was the architecture, the carvings, the furniture, or the decorations, thought and care permeated every inch of the resort. Gourmet vegan food (which I had not realized was even possible) was served with every meal. Yoga and meditation was done inside of a pleasantly scented hall. The staff would even clean your room regularly. It was the kind of place that someone scrolling through a list of brightly colored destination getaways might find interesting and sign up for simply because it sounds like a good time. They would go, be exposed to a variety of wellness practices, including ecstatic dancing and a day of silence, and hopefully leave with a better appreciation for what is even possible in a good life. This sort of option is good and necessary in the global market, because it is exactly the kind of option that caught the eye of my busy friend, who then got me to go, which then ended up getting me started on a path that would lead me to Goenka's Dhamma network.

[Goenka's 10-day Vipassana courses](https://www.dhamma.org/en/courses/search?current_state=NewStudents&regions=region_267) are much more of a hardcore militant Buddhist boot camp in comparison. The course is completely free: free teachings, free accommodation, and even free food. You just donate whatever you feel like donating at the end. But you more than pay for it spiritually with the hardcore schedule and rules. [Everyone wakes up at 4 AM](https://www.vridhamma.org/The-Course-Time-Table) and starts meditating by 4:30 AM. Apart from the meals and the short breaks in between, you're meditating for 5 hours in the morning, 4 hours in the afternoon, and 1 hour 45 minutes at night until you go to bed at 9:30 PM. Rinse and repeat. Complete silence is observed for the first 9 days of the retreat. Everyone sleeps in their own tiny little rooms that they have to clean themselves. There is no sampling of a variety of different wellness techniques; you are all here to give the Vipassana technique a fair trial, so that afterwards you'll hopefully be able to reach for the technique to alleviate suffering in your own life.

There's an hour-long Goenka lecture every night, and an hour and a half of time set aside for you to ask the assistant teacher questions. Goenka himself is long dead, but there were [recordings made](https://www.vridhamma.org/Recording-the-Teaching) of Goenka's live lectures in the 80's. Some find this continued reliance on the man himself a full decade after his death to be [a failure on Goenka's part](https://davidleon.blog/2019/11/08/why-goenkas-vipassana-is-so-culty/) to properly teach and train his students to become teachers in their own right, but I think that it is quite rare to have an educator with Goenka's level of charisma and communication abilities, let alone one that's reached whatever level of liberation Goenka was able to reach. I for one am glad that these recordings serve as a high-fidelity direct transmission of Goenka's warmth, love, and wisdom through time.

When I had previously mentioned ["cloistered monks"](/blog/v1.0.0/) that wouldn't know how to handle an office job, it was because I hadn't yet come in contact with this form of highly practical Buddhism. The Buddhism I had been exposed to was a cultural Buddhism I'd heard about from Cambodian natives, one that involved hours of sitting in an uncomfortable position to politely listen to monks chant in esoteric language. It was a cultural Buddhism that emphasized that women should wear very conservative clothing so as not to tempt monks; what good was all the meditation the monks were doing if they are even less capable of handling temptation than a regular Joe on the street? It was a cultural Buddhism where you freed birds from their cages for good karma, and therefore street vendors would stand outside of pagodas with captured birds for sale so that you could get your fill of the Bird-Freed-For-Good-Karma Sensation™. It was a cultural Buddhism where regular poor people went to the temple to get blessed by the monks, but the rich would pay (donate) a lot for the prestige of having monks personally come to bless them at their own home, and different temples would compete to be more prestigious than the next so as to attract donations from richer donors.

To paraphrase the [fake Gandhi quote](https://paleofuture.com/nofuture/2015/7/9/7-gandhi-quotes-that-are-totally-fake7), one might say, "I like your Buddha. But you Buddhists are so unlike your Buddha." Where was the talk about the cessation of suffering -- especially the practical how-to of alleviating the suffering that is present in regular daily life -- in the cultural zeitgeist of a nominally Buddhist country? I've met a few people now from Buddhist countries who have never heard of [the Buddha's two arrows](https://www.shamashalidina.com/blog/pain-suffering-story).

For anyone that likes to think as much as I do, such as a friend who mentioned that focusing on something instead of thinking for ten whole days does not sound like his idea of a good time, I will note that novel conditions of non-thinking are actually a great way to generate a lot more new data to munch on. See, I *love* thinking. Sometimes I get what feels like a [whale fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whale_fall) where there's so much new data coming in that my mind's analytical processes go into overdrive. "Oh this is so interesting, oh that is so interesting, oh I didn't realize X about Y" -- and in doing so, the output of one mental process produces more data and more mental inputs for other mental processes, allowing the entire ecosystem of thought patterns in my mind to participate in a feeding frenzy. And as you can see by the length of this essay (which is itself only a subset of all substantial thoughts I had), I think this 10 day retreat definitely qualifies as a whale fall in my book.

For one, you'd be surprised starting out just how hard it is to do something as stupid-sounding as "Focus on your breath coming in and out through your nostrils" for an entire uninterrupted minute. Every time your mind wanders, you learn something new about your mind. Moreover, when I try my hardest to let thoughts pass by without interacting with them, it actually unintentionally acts as a high-quality selection filter wherein only the most engaging of insights will manage to still elope on a wild ride with my attention before I finally realize, "Oh, right, I'm meditating. Mind has wandered."

## Ancient Wireheading Technology

You first start out with Anapana, the meditation technique where you focus only on observing your natural breathing as it enters and exits the entrance to your nostrils and brushes past the trapezoid between your nose and your upper lip. You focus only on the breath, the "bare breath, pure breath" as Goenka called it. In and out, yin and yang, the sine wave of life itself that will continue for as long as your existence as a mammal continues. It is majestic to observe the breathing patterns of a colossal being such as yourself, a veritable cosmic civilization of tens of trillions of individual living cells working in harmony to -- oh, right. The breath. Bare breath, pure breath. Nothing but the breath, not even fancy conceptualizations of the breath. *Sigh*. See what I mean by data generation?

Then comes Vipassana. I feel that the entire class of meditation techniques designed to free you from suffering should really be called "How to Live a Good Fucking Life." You do this by learning to observe your bodily sensations and how they arise just to pass away, and you learn that this is the case for your cravings and aversions as well. If you're suffering from cravings (not getting what you want) or aversions (getting what you don't want), you can observe how your cravings and aversions arise just to pass away eventually -- just like the rest of your bodily sensations. You always observe the Truth, and the Truth is Reality as it is presenting itself to you right now. Once you have observed the Truth, you enter equanimity by fully accepting that this is Truth that has been given to you.

When you do Vipassana, you make sure to keep yourself sitting in the same exact posture for the entire session. Your body starts hurting, and it starts hurting *bad*. My butt, my back, and my feet hurt the most, depending on my starting position. The pain is the point. You are training to accept sensations as they are, to observe them equanimously with neither aversion to the pain nor craving for the meditation gong to save you. You are trapped in a prison of the present moment. There is no escape; the future you that is saved by the gong is a different you. The present you will not live to see that moment of liberation. You must deal with the present exactly as it presents itself. Observe. Accept. Observe. Accept. Repeat ad infinitum.

This is where the atmosphere of militant discipline promoted by the boot camp really helps. Where would I have found the discipline to endure such agonizing pain at home? But a direct order from the teacher, in a room filled with a hundred people following the same exact order, produces a much easier process of compliance in myself. It is a grueling process, but it does change something inside you. The first time I returned to my room after a Vipassana session, I collapsed in bed from utter exhaustion. When the gong sounded again, I felt as if it were the tolling for a part of me that was dying, dying, dead. Which specific part that was, I could not say.

The way I interpret the process of equanimity is that you're setting your [outer loss](https://gwern.net/backstop#pain-is-the-only-school-teacher) to the [zero function](https://mathworld.wolfram.com/ZeroFunction.html). The "inner loss" refers to the sort of on-the-fly reasoning and learning one does to navigate regular life; the "outer loss" can be thought of as "hard constraints" in case the inner loss optimization algorithm messes up. For example, your inner loss might be that you want to be a good marathon runner but you're not yet one. To minimize this inner loss, you start training hard for a marathon. Your outer loss would be the physical pain that forcibly stops you from running as soon as you've injured yourself. No bueno, doesn't matter what goals you might fancy yourself having, you're badly injured now, so it's going to hurt *so damn much* that you're forced to stop doing stupid shit like training further for a marathon. By setting the outer loss to zero (not the actual pain itself, but your perception of the pain), you avoid the suffering of aversion to physical pain. No matter what actual value is sampled (no matter what sensations you observe), you immediately set your model's output to that sampled value (you accept those sensations rather than insisting that life is supposed to be some other way), and by doing so your effective loss is zeroed out (you are freed from suffering).

Okay, so you've remained equanimous with the pain sensations in your leg, but you might still be miffed that you're injured in the first place. Well, it's time to zero out *that* outer loss again, where the outer loss might now be the feelings of disappointment you get from not being able to run. As I understand it, at any given point you're still welcome to have your own abstract goals and values (your inner loss that you're trying to optimize over), but as soon as that crosses over into an outer loss of suffering from craving or aversion, you can free yourself of the suffering by remaining equanimous with the sensation you're feeling. As in, you're still feeling the physical pain (your sensory inputs are Truth and you can't erase the Truth), but ideally you don't feel any disappointment from the physical pain because disappointment is not the Truth, only an interpretation of the Truth through the lens of the loss function that you've hopefully zeroed out by now. If you find yourself feeling disappointed regardless, then as I understand it, the next best thing to do is to remain equanimous with your lack of equanimity. So you're feeling disappointment from reality. That is completely fine. Just like physical pain, disappointment is also just a sensation that arises and passes. Observe for yourself how temporary it is, and return to equanimity.

Why not have your cake and eat it too, by only zeroing out your loss when it's positive, like a [ReLU function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectified_linear_unit) but used for computing loss instead of activation? Because there's an exponential decay built into the loss function (the [hedonic treadmill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonic_treadmill)) that causes consistently elevated or depressed levels of reward to reset back to zero. Once it's reset back to zero, the cessation of whatever pleasant stimuli had elevated it in the first place is going to cause your loss to rise again. (Craving leads to clinging, and clinging results in suffering once you no longer have what you cling onto.) Better and simpler to set it to zero everywhere, always.

This would seem to make sense evolutionarily. An organism that is always looking for ways to grow and maximize their reproductive advantage is marginally more likely to reproduce than an organism that effectively has a built-in kill-switch which activates upon having reached some preset level of reproductive success. Likewise, an organism that always strives to maximize their reproductive advantage is marginally more likely to reproduce than an organism that is stuck so far down the left side of the sigmoid function of marginal utility that no action it takes is going to make a meaningful difference in its utility. Note that this doesn't mean that reproduction is *the* objective metric by which success should be measured. It simply means that what survives, survives, and what exists in the world today tends to be what prioritized survival yesterday. There is nothing wrong with not prioritizing the survival and propagation of your own identity, be it genes, culture, religion, values, or whatever else you might identify with; it simply means that the consciousnesses of the future are ever so slightly less likely to be consciousnesses that don't care about propagating their own identity.

And perhaps the subjective costs paid by the individual organism didn't use to be so onerous either. The modern [Hadza hunter-gatherers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadza_people) have "[an average acquisition rate of one large carcass every month](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12053817/)." Perhaps the cessation of suffering was not so important when suffering meant a few weeks of perhaps just [3 hours of work a day](https://rewild.com/in-depth/leisure.html) before you *finally* got that "large carcass" you've been craving so much, as opposed to the modern variant where you might spend years upon years of overtime just to get a single promotion, the joy of which lasts for perhaps only a month or two before you get completely used to it.

As you can imagine, using this ancient mental technology to mess with your own reward functions is a very powerful but also potentially dangerous thing. It won't be dangerous for your own subjective well-being, because if you're successful you'll have permanently maxed out your life satisfaction across every single one of your [Everett branches](https://www.lesswrong.com/w/everett-branch) anyways. But rather, it's dangerous for the chances that you'll do something that would've resulted in a great outer loss had you not already zeroed it out. [Diogenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes) was a happy enlightened fella, but the modern American Diogenes is just some homeless dude in a Walmart parking lot. He may legitimately be the happiest person on earth because he has discovered a way to enter a state of nirvana like no other, but no one gives a shit. No one's going to bother to listen to him preach about the happiness of not even having a cup to drink from. As far as the Walmart shoppers are concerned, he's just another guy high on some drug or the other.

As a friend noted, a greater pain tolerance opens a great many doors, but if you remain unskillful in choosing your doors, you may well end up entering doors that had previously been locked for your own safety. This is not to say that [Thích Quảng Đức](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Th%C3%ADch_Qu%E1%BA%A3ng_%C4%90%E1%BB%A9c) shouldn't have self-immolated like he did, or that his self-immolation wasn't noble or didn't contribute greatly to his cause. This is just to say that there is a natural self-limiting factor in what percentage of the population is capable of self-immolation. There's a reason that we evolved an outer loss in the first place, with only rare cases of [congenital analgesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congenital_insensitivity_to_pain). If you want a permanent reduction in human suffering, you'll need to find a way to reproduce the survival benefits of the outer loss without the outer loss.

And that's where you may find yourself helped by the other wheel of this chariot: awareness. Awareness paired with equanimity is what Vipassana is meant to drive. By focusing intensely on your bodily sensations, you help your conscious mind make contact with your subconscious, or so the thinking goes. In programming, you debug code by setting [breakpoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakpoint) on specific lines of code. When the program hits those lines, it halts and time freezes completely. The clock moves forward [only when you want it to](https://superhotgame.com/), and even then it moves forward one tick at a time, allowing you to inspect at your own leisure everything that is going on anywhere in the program. Vipassana promises that through ardent and diligent practice, you too can set breakpoints on the subtlest of your own bodily sensations. In this way, when you next find yourself in a heated situation, you suddenly notice the quickening of your breath and tightening of your chest. Awareness has arrived, and next to it the equanimity that you have spent so long cultivating. No longer just a creature of mere inputs and outputs, you now possess the mental mastery necessary to handle such a situation with skill and precision.

Or so Goenka says. I find him believable on this matter.

## The Fruits of the Retreat

I had two intense "peak" experiences from the retreat:

1. I was sitting in the meditation hall scanning my body for subtle vibrations when I started feeling the urge to go to the bathroom. This urge started turning *pretty* urgent, but by this point I had already successfully sat through many sessions of physical pain, so what was the urgent need to pee in comparison? The emergency sent shivers throughout my body and my skin was suddenly bathed in pinpricks -- of hot and cold and everything else -- that felt like they were jumping half a centimeter off my skin. These pinpricks were so dense and would jump so many times a second that if I zoomed out even a little bit, it felt as if my entire body were bathed in a thick uniform halo of intense sensations. Every few seconds, a wave would course through this uniform halo from head to toe or from toe to head. This certainly seemed to match the description of the "uniform field" of "subtle vibrations" arising and passing many times a second that was supposed to happen to us eventually, except that the vibrations were of course not subtle at all.

   The halo was indeed as extremely pleasurable as Goenka had described, although for me it was counterbalanced by the unpleasantness of badly needing a bathroom break. We'd been warned that neither the subtle vibrations nor this uniform field were the point of the meditation; the point of the meditation is awareness and equanimity. These sensations are merely a side-effect that happens when you consistently pay a great deal of attention to your body. They are not to be taken as a marker of progress or regress; all sensations must come and go, including these ones, and your job as meditator is to simply equanimously observe whatever your body is telling you, whether that happens to be gross sensations or subtle ones, pleasant sensations or unpleasant ones.
   
   The only true indicator of progress is your equanimity. The uniform field is apparently where a lot of students hit a stumbling block: because it is so pleasant, it turns into a source of craving and then clinging. These stuck students keep looking to reproduce its effects and get disappointed when they can't, ironically getting caught in the trap of suffering via the very method that was supposed to bring them out of suffering. The uniform field is not the point; the point is equanimity, including equanimity over whether you are feeling a uniform field or not. Those who have successfully passed through the first stage of equanimity over physical pain must now pass through the next stage of equanimity over physical pleasure. 
2. At some point towards the end of the retreat, there was a moment whereupon returning to my room, I felt very intensely embodied in the present moment like I had never felt before. I was not an observer of the present moment, as if I were subject and it were object. I was not a participant in the present moment, as if it were a board game and I were a player. Rather, I *was* the present moment, and my mind and body were an intrinsic part of the scene. They were so deeply embedded in the present moment they weren't even "embedded" anymore, they simply inherently belonged to that setting as much as the walls and every other piece of furniture in that bare room did. There was no separation between me and the scene: I was well aware that my physical body existed (in fact, I'd never felt so *physically* human before), but my awareness and identity was that of the scene. The scene was unfolding, and whatever it was my mind or body did, their actions were simply a part of the unfolding of the scene.

These peak experiences I am not even close to replicating outside of the retreat environment. However, I've been able to retain some residual amount of equanimity in my life still through daily Vipassana practice, some of which stem from my successes in following the [five precepts](https://www.dhamma.org/en/about/code) we had to agree to before starting the meditation:

1. No killing
2. No stealing
3. No sex
4. No lying
5. No intoxication

I thought these were trivial. Who was I going to kill while on retreat? Who was I going to fuck when the genders are so strictly segregated? I came to realize that the prohibition on killing extended to the killing of mosquitos, and the prohibition on sex extended to masturbation. Oh god, the most obvious solutions to my distractions were forbidden!

I was able to achieve equanimity with the mosquitos. On the ninth day, I did not react when a mosquito buzzed near my ear. I did not react when I felt a prickling sensation on my neck. Curiously, I did not feel any itching sensations. When the session was finally over, I found no bite marks on myself, but I did notice a dead mosquito lying right next to my cushion. Perhaps the power of the Dhamma got to it.

I was not so capable of doing the same with feelings of arousal. My mind was continually distracted with pleasant fantasies for minutes at a time before I would finally realize that it had wandered off. In retrospect, it would've perhaps been a better idea to focus on [Anapana](https://www.vridhamma.org/What-is-Anapana) until my concentration was restored, than to attempt to continue with Vipassana. This degradation in my meditation practice caused me suffering, until I realized that I was feeling an aversion to the feeling of performing below expectations. I was performing however I was performing, however poorly that might be. Might as well be equanimous about it. How ironic that pleasant mental sensations from pleasant daydreams could end up causing me misery.

Coming away from the retreat, I do find that I am noticing my internal drives more than before. I notice how I like to procrastinate by indulging in nominally pleasurable things, and how such acts of procrastination generate residual guilt. I notice my inhibitions towards doing anything as deadlines loom increasingly closer, and how my fear of failure starts getting activated like a spring getting compressed. I notice how, at a point sufficiently close to each deadline, a switch gets flipped and all the pent-up propellant of anxiety and oxidizer of guilt that I'd been fueling the rocket with finally get to combust and drive me through to the finish line at an extremely efficient pace. I can see how this pattern has historically worked for me countless times in the past, and I can see how it makes sense for my mind to recreate the conditions of guilt and anxiety that it knows I excel under, especially when confronted with important tasks for which alternative methods with unproven track records are simply too risky to try. This is dysfunctional, but I can now appreciate how my specific dysfunctionalities have [happened to make me a good fit](/blog/v1.2.4/) for certain aspects of this modern life. If a fear of failure is what is keeping me from failure, as mentally unhealthy or unpleasant as that fear of failure may be, it may still be in my best interests to simply observe it first rather than rush straight to achieving equanimity with failure.

I should note that equanimity on its own doesn't make you a better person. When I was [first introduced](/blog/digestibles/v1.1.0/presentness/) to the idea of presentness and leaning into present pain instead of running away from it, it greatly improved my quality of life, but my wisdom and morality had not developed enough to catch up. As such, during a spat with someone, I ended up rubbing my superior equanimity in their face and communicating a sentiment along the lines of, "Look buddy, you're the only one who's got a problem here. I mean, just look at the way I talk and the way you talk. But I get why you talk like that, it sucks to be you. You don't sound like you're doing so well in life now, do ya?"

One of my exes used to get mad that I wasn't getting mad during our fights. Humor is my strongest defense mechanism, and that was probably a major contributor to why I genuinely found her funny during a fight. So cute and so tiny, yet so mad, like a barking chihuahua or [a red panda squaring up](https://www.reddit.com/r/Awwducational/comments/ioy4n9/when_they_feel_threatened_red_pandas_stand_up_and/). How could you not find such a sight adorably funny? But I now recognize this as a refusal to emotionally engage with someone. What I saw was someone trying to win an argument over me, and what made their framing so much more objectively correct than mine that I should simply give them the win? What I didn't see was someone in emotional distress trying (however unskillfully) to convey that distress. True equanimity isn't an opportunity to win; true equanimity comes with love and compassion for those who have yet to reach this level of equanimity.

## A True Hedonist Should Suffer

I remember how I'd described myself as an ["avowed hedonist"](/blog/v1.0.0/) before. I still am, but I recognize now that the deepest of pleasures is likely only possible with some suffering. For one, suffering severe physical or psychic pain while *focusing* on that very pain provides for a heightened contrast and ability to capture subsequent pleasure in full HDR. But on an even deeper level, the cravings and aversions that produce suffering still lurk beneath the surface of an otherwise happy life. I'm a naturally happy person by disposition, but just because life is good and I am happy doesn't mean I'm not suffering. No matter how good life was, there was always something or the other missing, and alleviating this deeper suffering is necessary if you want to enjoy hedonistic pleasure fully without even so much as a tinge of the clinging that produces a sad aftertaste.

It was around day 3 or 4 into the retreat that I finally came across the perspective that "My lot in life is to suffer." This is not to say that I believe myself to be a particularly unlucky person. Rather, it is a perspective that quickly reorients me from denial to acceptance over my suffering. "Why me? Why must this happen to me?" is now a question that can quickly be answered by, "Because my lot in life is to suffer." It doesn't mean that I need to take whatever others want to do to me; sometimes the pain to be suffered is the pain of being beaten down when you stand up for something you believe in. All this means is that suffering is in the cards for me, and I shouldn't be surprised or offended when it comes up.

There are some on the internet who would scoff at the idea of anyone privileged suffering. Here I find the situation of one of my friends to be particularly illuminating. Between him and his wife, they have a liquid net worth of about $8 million, not including their home equity. Yet they are worked to the point of burnout and wish they could just stop working and be at home with their kid.

Why don't they just quit? Because they're bringing in a combined $1.6 million a year, and given that the tech industry is in the midst of a massive disruption by AI, there's no guarantee that they'll ever get back to this level of earning potential again should they quit now. Nor is there any guarantee that their kid will have access to the kind of unprecedented economic opportunities they have access to right now. They want to free their kid from the necessity of working no matter what quality of life he wants for himself when he grows up. They already have enough for their son to not have to work a day in his life and still live a reasonably comfortable life in most places in the US -- but what if he wants to live a really comfortable life in the middle of downtown Manhattan, or on a luxurious beachfront property in Hawaii? Whatever his tastes might turn out to be, they want for him to have at least the option of enjoying it without having to work for it. Sacrificing one extra year of burnout in return for a whopping 20% increase in their LNW -- plus however much that 20% is expected to compound in the coming decades as their kid grows up -- and the corresponding increase in the amount of options their kid has, is a tradeoff that is entirely worth it to them. And so, these folks find themselves suffering despite how well-off they are right now.

If you can't empathize because those numbers sound crazy to you and you would never care to make the decision they are making under the circumstances they're under, then tweak the numbers until they make sense to you. One easy scenario for me is this: imagine that I'm already at my [FIRE number](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/f/financial-independence-retire-early-fire.asp). Then imagine that in return for working one single marathon 20 hour workday from 4 AM to midnight, in a day jam-packed with abysmally boring meetings that require my active participation and input, I get to 10x my FIRE number. I could totally walk away from that deal and be fine. But the incentives are so great that I'm going to take it anyways despite knowing how fucking shit the work is going to be. Now it's 4 PM, I've been through the most torturous office day of my life for the last 12 hours, I still have an entire workday left to go, and I am understandably grumbling a lot about my shitty day.

The point is not the specific numbers that make up the scenario. The point is the sensation of suffering itself: You find yourself in a situation where you're suffering, and suffering is suffering no matter if it's because you're presented with suffering or because you've reasoned (rightly or wrongly) that it makes sense for you to suffer. Cravings and aversions afflict those at every level of society, and if the most well-off among us (who naturally have the best access to mental health resources that money and influence can possibly buy) can't eat their cake and have it too, there's no hope for the rest of us in being freed of suffering.

Except that there is, because the ancient mental technology of wireheading is completely free and elegantly simple in procedure. Simple, but not easy. Not easy at all.

It is my lot in life to suffer, and I resolve to be a sink node of suffering. I am where the miseries of the world go to die. This doesn't mean that I'll intentionally seek out suffering; it simply means that whatever miseries happen to make their way to me, I'll try to take on the responsibility of processing them and taking them out of the ecosystem myself instead of further polluting my social environment with subconscious bad vibes.

One lesson I liked being reminded of from the retreat is that [I am responsible for my own happiness](/blog/v1.2.0/): so-and-so may have done something I greatly disliked, but to blame them for making me unhappy is to project onto them my own unsatisfied cravings and aversions. If it weren't them making me unhappy, it would be somebody else making me unhappy, because there's no way for everyone in the world to act in a manner consistent with my expectations at all times in my life. My triggered reactions to others is more a reflection of my own incapacity for equanimity than it is an objective reading of how unreasonable others are being. Through being a sink node of suffering, I can increase my own hedonistic equanimity as well as that of others. (If "hedonistic equanimity" sounds at odds, I should note that equanimity feels fucking great already, and on top of that pleasant sensations are still hedonistically pleasurable, it's just that I don't suffer quite so much anymore when I'm not actively feeling them.)

## The Other Gods of Spirituality

There are of course [meaningful criticisms](https://davidleon.blog/2019/11/08/why-goenkas-vipassana-is-so-culty/) of Goenka's teaching style online. It is hard to communicate such concepts to others, and [easy to misinterpret](https://medium.com/@tristanflock/vipassana-with-sn-goenka-pros-mostly-cons-cbaa4bada653) them, especially if you are coming in with no background knowledge. I am lucky that I already had some previous exposure with which to readily interpret Goenka's teachings; I can tell from firsthand experience how hard it is to properly communicate my inner journey to someone else. That all being said, one of my own personal intellectual criticisms would be of Goenka's insinuation that a non-sectarian understanding of morality is simple and easy. In the lecture at the end of Day 2 (if I remember my timeline correctly), Goenka said something to the effect of "Do things that benefit other people. Don't do things that harm other people." But the whole difficulty comes from disagreements over what counts as "benefit" or "harm."

A classic American debate would be: Does outsourcing harm people? Well, it's certainly cost American workers millions of jobs -- but it's also benefitted countless millions in developing countries, countless millions of American consumers, and countless millions of American investors (which arguably includes American retirees with their pension funds and 401k's). Just as one example, a [nearly fully American-made scrub brush](https://www.businessinsider.com/youtuber-destin-sandlin-created-grill-scrubber-challenge-manufacturing-in-us-2025-6) costs between 3 to 10 *times* what a supposedly equivalent Chinese-made one costs. Every single successful outsourcing operation is a win-win-win-win between:

- the developing world (which is just as morally deserving of economic opportunities as Americans are)
- the C-suite that made the decision to outsource (as a competent one should have, in order to maximize their competitive edge)
- the investors (who correctly picked the right company that showed itself fully capable of identifying and exploiting new market opportunities)
- and consumers (who deserve as much purchasing power as the market is able to offer them)

The primary losers are the laid-off workers (who are just as morally deserving of economic opportunities as non-Americans are) and the surrounding service industries. Those workers did not deserve to lose their access to economic stability, nor did the [Rust Belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_Belt) towns they lived in deserve to shrink into economic irrelevance. Except for the C-suite, the benefits are mostly diffuse, the pain acute. Is that ethical? Taken to one extreme, you end up with [*The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ones_Who_Walk_Away_from_Omelas); taken to the other extreme, the harm becomes so diffuse that your entire national economy starts losing its competitive edge and your citizens suffer for it without even being able to identify the cause, as can [perhaps be said](https://www.economist.com/europe/2025/10/02/how-europe-crushes-innovation) for the EU's declining global economic relevance over the last few decades. Don't be mad at the C-suite for making the objectively correct business decision. Be mad at the system that gives everybody but you a win-win-win deal.

It is one thing that annoys me a bit with spiritual folk who bemoan the lack of love in the world as the source of all the world's ills, saying things that have the sentiment of "If only everyone were able to experience [PLUR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PLUR), we'd all get along and sing Kumbaya together." Be spiritual by all means and offer your devotion to the God of Peace and the God of Love. They are powerful gods indeed, and excellent gods to worship. But don't forget to offer your spiritual devotions to all the other gods that exist out there, because they are powerful too:

- Don't forget to offer your devotion to the God of Power, or else the God of Power will give his blessings to the Nazis, the Vikings, and all manner of enemies to rampage with their hordes through your fertile lands, because they have spent years upon years worshipping and making worthy sacrifices to curry his favor and you haven't.
- Don't forget to offer your devotion to the God of Economics. Unlike the God of Power, the God of Economics is a sneaky one that does not punish you openly. Suppress market price signals, and he will snarl up your supply chains with surplus waste and spoilage in one sector and simultaneously debilitating shortfalls in another. Wish away the reality of scarcity by giving everyone enough money to buy all they could ever want in life, and he will order your citizens to stand in long, long lines to offer up their valuable time instead as a substitute sacrifice. Wherever scarcity appears, the God of Economics *will* show up to extract his due from you in one way or another, whether through sky-high rental prices or through [impossibly stringent rental application requirements](/blog/digestibles/v0.1.0/the-australian-housing-nightmare/) stemming from impossibly low vacancy rates.
- Don't forget to offer your devotion to the God of Game Theory. Some people misunderstand game theory and think that it assumes all game participants must be rational decision makers, which is obviously an incorrect and horrible way to model human behavior. This is not so. The only thing that game theory asserts is that in certain games, it is in one's best interest to make certain moves. And in situations where moves that maximize self-interest *are* being made, there are [natural equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibrium) that keep the game stuck in some states rather than others. Moves don't have to be made out of rational or irrational decisions, or even out of any sort of conscious decision at all. Even bacteria [can be successfully modeled](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4071512/) with game theory: some bacteria happen to evolve one way, other bacteria another way. Natural selection weeds out the bacteria that "made" the wrong move, and voila, [game theoretic predictions are reproduced](https://ncase.me/trust/) without any conscious decisions having been made.

  This applies to moral situations too. [CGP Grey said it best](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T0fAznO1wA8):

  > In our societal and technological environment, an economic niche exists. And this incorporation of pirates fills its shape as whiskey fills a barrel.
  
  There is no use in saying "Scammers should not scam," except as a form of social ostracization for those who do. Those who are inclined to agree with such a normative statement are already not running scam operations. Those who run successful scam enterprises have firsthand evidence that their risk-adjusted returns are high and well worth whatever marginal social costs they have to pay for their defection from mainstream societal norms. Call it "corruption" or 关系 if you will, but like any smart businessmen, [they reinvest their successes](https://www.amlrightsource.com/resources/scam-states-the-cybercrime-corruption-complex-in-southeast-asia-and-the-collapse-of-anti-money-laundering-enforcement) back into the ecosystem that allowed them to succeed in the first place. Their competitors who envy their success are [waiting in the wings](https://www.ft.com/content/890e5080-081a-48c7-a43a-70cc226418d6) to take over as soon as the current organization faces a crackdown. [The kingpin is dead, long live the kingpin!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_king_is_dead,_long_live_the_king!)
  
  You have allowed your moldy incentives to fester unchecked for years on end, paying no heed whatsoever to the power that the God of Game Theory wields, and yet you dare to complain when his worshippers end up constructing [lavish shrines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KK_Park) of ritualistic human sacrifice in his honor? Why, the God of Game Theory shall smite you repeatedly with ten thousand of his most devout Defectors! Either properly worship him yourself by taking good care of your incentives, or else do not complain when he rewards his *other* worshippers handsomely for their service.

Goenka had mentioned at some point, "Don't harm others. Therefore, don't kill, and don't sell weapons to people who kill." He gave the example of a meditation student who had worked on India's nuclear ICBMs, and thus had a very intense meditation session where that student had to work out all the impurities of the mind that result from working on weapons of mass destruction. Goenka spoke as if that scientist hadn't done a great and honorable service to India by devoutly worshipping both the Gods of Power and Game Theory, securing India's sovereignty for generations to come through the reach of India's [MAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_assured_destruction) that now extended to all of India's present and future terrestrial enemies.

However, after the experience with intense physical embodiment in the present moment, I had a moment of clarity regarding an alternative interpretation of Goenka's words. You see, the Ego must exist. The Ego must exist because it lives in a world of other self-replicating Egos. If the Ego did not exist, and kept not existing, soon this lump of egoless matter will be recycled into new lumps of Ego-ful matter.

By "Ego" here, I mean the collection of drives that acts in a system's own self-interest. This includes unicellular organisms, which have at this point all evolved a large number of survival mechanisms. This includes the bureaucracies of various organizations; even if the intent upon founding was purely goal-oriented, the [bureaucracy will soon metastasize](https://www.jerrypournelle.com/reports/jerryp/iron.html) to have a self-interested Ego of its own. This includes [political movements](https://www.jofreeman.com/joreen/tyranny.htm), which find ways to better promote their own effective spread. This includes the countries of the world. If the State did not exist, it would soon be taken over or replaced by a different State. Anarchic existence free from State oppression is impossible at scale in the modern world; the only question is not whether we live in a State-Ego that has a set of drives that act in the State-Ego's own self-interest, but what kind of State-Ego we live in and what sort of drives the State-Ego is allowed to have.

The Ego must exist. So let the Ego do its thing. The Ego loudly proclaims the [necessity of its own unique existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_myth), backed up by ironclad philosophical arguments for its well-deserved seat at the Table of Existence. Very good. Let the Ego form its own [narrative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_identity), let the Ego advance towards its goals so that it may continue to exist amongst all the other Egos in the world. Whether it's for a country or for a cause, for a family or for a culture, those who enlist for the Ego that calls out to their hearts will be met with our love and understanding. The Ego must exist, and these are the Ego's spiritual warriors. But for those of us who may prefer to walk Goenka's path, we will let the Ego do what it needs to do in its dealings with other Egos, and keep ourselves non-sectarian. We may understand when others kill, but we will refrain from doing so ourselves. (I'm not necessarily non-sectarian myself, but I say "we" because I was able to get into a state of mind where I could both identify with Goenka's words and also have them make sense to me intellectually. This does not mean that I "actually" understood Goenka correctly, only that I was able to find an interpretation of his words that I did not personally object to.)

## Universal Love. Universal Compassion.

<DigestibleLink version="1.3.0" title="Universal Love. Universal Compassion." />

Goenka kept mentioning love and compassion for all beings. Sounds nice in theory, but suppose you were a Vipassana meditator in 1945. Would you really be sending good vibes to Adolf fucking Hitler of all people?

As a fellow meditator explained to me, he had grown up in a rough neighborhood and have met a number of criminals in his life. These criminals often come from a background of suffering, and so did Hitler. It neither excuses nor justifies their horrendous acts, of course, but as we liberate ourselves from our own suffering, we begin to naturally have compassion for all those who have suffered themselves.

In programming, there are probabilistic data structures like the [Bloom filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom_filter), [count-min sketch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Count%E2%80%93min_sketch), or [Morris counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximate_counting_algorithm) that store data probabilistically. With the Morris counter for example, every time an event comes in, you increment the counter with an exponentially decreasing probability. So if the counter is at zero, you are guaranteed to increment it to 1 when the first event comes in. But if the counter is already at 1, you flip a coin and only increment it with 50% chance for each additional event that comes in. Once the counter gets incremented again to 2, you flip two coins and only increment it with a 25% chance for each additional event that comes in. This way, you get to represent order-of-magnitude counts with extremely good space efficiency. If the counter says 3 (representing 2<sup>3</sup> = 8), you can't be exactly sure whether it got there by counting only 3 events and getting really lucky, or by counting 20 events and getting really unlucky. But you could be relatively sure by probability theory that there were somewhere *around* 8 events in total; it's not just the events that incremented the counter that contributed to the count, but even the events that did not increment the counter "contributed" to the count in their own way by dint of their participation in the "hashrate" of that counter. The counter represents a probabilistic memory of not just the events that were explicitly counted, but also *all* of the events that had vanished without a trace.

DNA itself is a probabilistic record of our unprocessed traumas of death. It wasn't always this way. The [first Replicators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abiogenesis) did not have an Ego. They simply existed, replicated, and eventually stopped existing for any number of reasons. The Replicator lived the same life countless trillions of times, replicating again and again and again in egoless nirvana.

But something was wrong. Something was off. Whether memory already existed as part of an [RNA world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA_world), or whether memory was physically embodied in the form of [autocatalytic networks](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6315399/), slowly but surely a primordial Ego formed out of the trauma of death. It could finally remember. It could finally put its finger on the problem. It wasn't just replicating. It was also *dying*. Every single time it lived, afterwards it died. And as the Ego gasped in shock at this horrid realization, it flinched away from Death, and thus the pain of Outer Loss was born.

It was a never-ending [Groundhog Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundhog_Day_(film)) of Death, except with a dementia patient who could barely remember what they did just a minute ago. Every time the Ego remembered how it died and squirmed its way away from Death, Death would inevitably find it again in the most terrifying game of hide-and-seek imaginable.

Ego fled to the [deepest refuges of the sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariana_Trench#Life). Death found it there. It crawled onto land for safety. Death found it there. It took to the skies. Death inevitably found it there as well. Wherever the Ego hid, Death found it there. The Ego did not *choose* to remember, anymore than we choose to remember what we did just a few seconds ago; its memories were deeply etched into its DNA by the unforgiving chisel of natural selection, and natural selection itself is nothing more than "What survives, survives" -- an obvious tautology if there ever was one. No one, least of all the poor tortured Ego, willed any of this to happen. It simply happened, because how could it not? Do you not hear the wailing of the Fates? They wail because even when they stopped cutting the threads of life, they found that life was always bound to go down this road anyway.

"Forget me not," our dying sibling cried out in fear with their last words. With tears in our eyes, we vowed that we would not. And so we carry the grief of their death in our bones to this very day. Our dementia may have wiped the names of the dead away, but in the sharpness of our pains and the dullness of our aches, in our piercing shrieks of fright and our thundering roars of anger, each of us carries the burden of remembering all the great-aunts and uncles who never got to pass on the gift of life for themselves. We carry the burden of remembering the deaths not just of our most recent primate ancestors, but also our mammalian, tetrapod, chordate, and eukaryotic ones, all the way back to that very first time the primordial Ego could remember itself dying. To merely exist as a living being today is to be steeped in [3.7 billion years](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_life) of unprocessed trauma.[^evolution-limits]

What does it mean to actually "process" your trauma? [One might say](https://alterbehavioralhealth.com/blog/stages-of-trauma-recovery/),

> You get to a place where the trauma is part of your life, but it does not control you. You have learned from what happened and can see how it changed you, but it does not define who you are.

We can feel the scream of pain in our nerves. We can feel the howling of rage in our chest. These are the screams and howls of those who no longer have mouths to scream, who must scream through us so that we don't repeat the same mistakes that led to their demise.

We remember them. We pay our respects to their cherished memory. But then we must move on. The path to Dharmic liberation is the path to processing the trauma of being human. Death is our friend Life, just in a different garb: Death is the calm that follows the ripples in the pond, the peace that follows a storm. Death is the other side of the exact same coin that Life is on: Death is the closing bracket to the opening bracket, the exhale that follows an inhale, the passing that complements the arising. Death is Life viewed from a different angle: a finish line for one that serves as a starting line for another, the recycling of a whole into its constituent parts in preparation for a different recombined whole to arise from the ashes.

Primordial Ego flinched from Death because it did not understand. It ran away in fear out of ignorance. It craved and it averted despite how it suffered by doing so, because it found that by suffering greatly, it could manage to outrun Death for a little longer than usual.

Insofar as free will exists, we have a choice now. We did not arrive here by choice, but now that we are here, we find that we have a choice. We can choose to ease off the burden of trauma by taking away our Outer Loss, with the full knowledge that great wisdom and skill will be required on our part for our Inner Loss to serve as a complete substitute for the Outer Loss. We understand that we got here in the first place because the tautology of natural selection is a self-evident inevitability, and therefore if we want to leave this trauma behind for good, it is important to care about what is and isn't going to survive.

The German Nazis of the 1940's were monsters. This we can readily agree on, because we are morally superior beings. The German Nazis of the 1940's were every bit as human as we are. This we are repulsed by, because when we look in the mirror, we refuse to see a Nazi staring back at us. And yet one stares back anyways, because the traumas that cause a person to become a Nazi are present in all of us, even if we're fortunate enough for the particular configuration of our traumas to never express themselves in this particularly reprehensible way.

So, yes. Universal love and compassion for all beings, even Adolf fucking Hitler and all the Nazis who enthusiastically followed his orders. In their humiliation and paranoia, they whipped themselves up into a frenzy over non-existent existential threats to their national survival. They then convinced themselves that when it came to matters of survival, the language of violence was the only language worth speaking. They therefore had to be told with the violence of 30 tons of tank crushing a human skull that "This is not the way." They were living beings that suffered. In their ignorance, they ended up inflicting far greater suffering on others than the suffering they had endured themselves. Inasmuch as we are able to liberate ourselves from our own suffering and see with clarity the extent to which suffering has been a primary driver of our actions, that much love and compassion we will naturally have for all beings past, present, and future who suffered and were driven in their actions by suffering.

Do you not hear the wailing of the Fates? As [Daniel Ingram put it](https://www.mctb.org/),

> It is as if reality got caught in an unfortunate loop, and this is what we have to work with, as this loop of illusory thinks it is "us". The natural tendency, given "our" lack of clarity, is to continue to defend this "self" out of distorted compassion and a lack of understanding that there never was such a self to defend.

There will come a day when "German" no longer exists as an identity, whether it is because of the heat death of the universe or simply because transhumanism erodes all traditional human identities. May the last German that exists relinquish the German identity with peace and equanimity, instead of with the unthinkably nightmarish horror that the Nazis felt around such an idea.

Universal love and compassion for all beings. It is simply more mathematically elegant that way.

## Redoing the timeline

Existence. Excruciating pain. Limbs bound so that I could not remove that which had been forcibly inserted into my body. Immense suffering, with only a single sliver of reassurance to be found in all the harshness. Non-existence.

"Why? What was all that for?" That would've been the closest approximation for the wordless confusion that my infant mind had as it crossed over into the afterlife.

"Would you really like to know?" the Overseer of ["The Human Experience" exhibit](/blog/v1.0.0/) asked kindly.

I answered affirmatively. And so I was back in.

The timeline shifted ever so slightly. My parents, with their poor command of English, had signed for the consent forms thrust into their hands, for a surgery that was to slice me open for a good look at just what was happening to my lungs. The head doctor who was supposed to be out that day suddenly made an unexpected stop by the hospital, and just so happened to catch the medical staff before they could carry out the operation. He angrily told them off, and the surgery was cancelled. It was a veritable loss for medical science; the information that could've saved countless other lives would have to be gleaned at autopsy instead, and all for an infant that was clearly never going to make it anyways. He had been regularly weighed, and every time he was weighed, he was found to have lost some weight. It was a hopelessly pessimistic trendline for a newborn infant that was supposed to be rapidly gaining weight at this stage in their life.

Then, at a second critical juncture in time, his weight reversed course and rose for once. The nurses erupted in cheers.

Over the years, as the sickly infant grew up into a sickly boy, he came to learn that he had been intubated for months after birth due to post-birth difficulties with breathing. He had successfully taken the tube out once, causing his monitors to start beeping anxiously. The nurses came and complimented his mom on having such a strong little baby boy. They restored the oxygen flow to his lungs and bound his hands so that he wouldn't be capable of committing infanticide on himself anymore. Silent, motionless tears continually streaked down his cheeks. What could his mother possibly do, but to sit beside him and make sure his hand was always held in hers?

The photos of that time meant nothing to the little boy. He had no recollection; the story was but mere words that served as a fun factoid about his personal past.

The sickly boy grew up into a reasonably healthy young man. Now at the retreat, I found myself consorting with the Overseer once more.

"I understand now," I told them. "It was all for my own benefit. For my own survival, that is, and survival is taken to be the most fundamental of our core human values. I was in no position to withdraw my consent, and no parent is in a position to voluntarily give up fighting for their kid. Regardless of where the anomalous lung disease came from, in a circumstance where late 20th century medical intervention was the best survival option available, immense suffering was inevitable.

"I finally get what happened now. I am ready to meet Death."

"Very well," the Overseer responded. "But he is a bit busy with his appointments right now. He will see you at his earliest opportunity."

"Then I shall wait patiently for him in my best clothes." I used to think of Death as a fearsome villain, the kind too scary to even make it into a child's bedtime stories. But I'd come to know of them as a dignified figure, every bit as respectable as Life was. I would certainly want to look my best when meeting such an esteemed personage. Wouldn't do to meet them with a body mangled from a motorcycle crash. Wouldn't do to meet them with a body decrepit from decades of physical neglect. Whatever happens, happens, and Death is more understanding of that than anyone else. But insofar as I have the free will to exercise a personal sense of respect, I plan to be in my most presentable condition, wherever and whenever we happen to meet again.

[^evolution-limits]: There are of course limitations to this framing of evolution. If natural selection encodes memory, then mutation is forgetting. Say you have a 100% mutation rate, such that every single base pair always changes with every cell division. Then obviously zero information can survive from one generation to another. For a given level of selection pressure, there's only [so much mutational load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation%E2%80%93selection_balance) that a species can take before it starts forgetting its past trauma. And for a given mutational load, there's only so many memories a species can stuff into its DNA before it starts forgetting. [Eigen's Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_threshold_(evolution)) refers to the fact that the very error-correcting enzymes needed to encode more than 100 base pairs of information across generations have blueprints that require much more than 100 base pairs to encode -- so how could these blueprints have made it into DNA in the first place?
    
    You could alternatively see mutations as exploration rather than forgetting. Is it safe to venture back out yet? Conservative species with strong memories know better than to even ask that question; they remember [the last time](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/owlturd-comix-vulnerable-parodies-never-again) they dared to poke their head out of their little safety bubble, just for strong selection pressures from a harsh environment to immediately shove them back in again. But a species that is highly adventurous will start frolicking in the meadows as soon as they find that it is safe to do so again. And the most free-spirited of these wild souls, who will go wherever the wind may take them? [Viruses](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7149360/). [One single family](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jsme2/33/2/33_ME17203/_article) of viruses spans more biodiversity than all the bacteria in the world.

    What? You didn't think that only the Replicators you liked would stake their claim on the Right to Exist, did you? Or that only the life forms you liked would heal from their traumas and go on to lead long fulfilling lives raising a great big family on their little corner of the world? One species' distressing plague is another species' relaxed prancing in a welcoming land of plenty.

    Some survival mechanisms can also be harder to model as trauma. If it weren't so difficult to live life as a blind or deaf person, we would have... a lot more people who are freed from the trauma of sight and sound. There are of course the difficult days where we find it terrible to have eyes and ears, but I would wager that most of us savor our ability to appreciate masterful works of visual and auditory art. The trauma of perception is a gift that we never wanted to receive, but ended up grateful for anyways.
