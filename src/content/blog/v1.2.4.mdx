I could not keep myself [immersed in passion](/blog/v1.2.3/) forever. As a mammal, I had to come up for air sooner or later, and when I did, I found that the habits that I had cultivated before were no longer there to support me.

Yet life was still fine. I got the sense that I wished for change yet again, but seeing as there was no urgency in leaving this state of being, I could take my time to observe what it was about this life that made the grass on the other side look greener:

- My constant flitting about from one thing to another meant that everything was left in a half-finished state. I finally realized at some point that literally everything in my life was in an uncomfortable state of incompletion. This was easy enough to adjust to. My life is incomplete. That's fine. ["My life is complete"](/blog/v1.1.0/) is a label and a narrative I can return back to at any time. It is now time to explore all the ways in which that narrative is not true.
- All the tasks that were pending completion collectively produced a sense of overwhelm and confusion. This took a little getting used to. ["One thing"](/blog/v1.2.3/) applies not just to the overarching theme of life, but also to life in the moment. Ruthless prioritization and focusing on one task at a time still gets things done by the time they need to be done.
- The long mental to-do list produced a constant state of rush. With this one, I benefitted from my recent experience around learning to drive a manual motorcycle. When your motorcycle is going just about as fast as it can go on its current gear, giving it even more throttle isn't going to help it move any faster. You need to pull the clutch in to disengage the engine from the transmission, shift up a gear, and then release the clutch to [reconnect the engine to the transmission](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=g8xnIFf4id4) at a shorter gear ratio so that every cycle of the engine translates into more ground traveled. Likewise, if I find myself not getting to everything I want to get to in a day despite having had a very busy day, the solution is not to push myself even harder, but instead to disengage, assess and prioritize, and re-engage. What I need is not more effort, but a better system for transmitting effort into results.

  (Fun note: I suppose this sense of rush is what some might call the ["white supremacist"](https://www.whitesupremacyculture.info/urgency.html) side of me. [The motte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motte-and-bailey_fallacy), as I understand it, is that individuals aren't actually trying to be racist when they've internalized a false sense of urgency, they're simply expressing the Europeanized culture that they've been brought up in. As Tema Okun says, this is an organizational mishap that "undermines us all," but one that disproportionately affects members of marginalized communities unaccustomed to the Protestant work ethic. The bailey is that I have actually seen this terminology unironically weaponized by a trans white feminist activist against a trans BIPOC feminist activist; the trans BIPOC feminist activist of course had their own cards to play to accuse the trans white feminist activist of white supremacy as well. Meanwhile there are folks out there with 1,488 swastika tattoos on them who would proudly tell you all about their membership in the superior white race.)
- Relations with the people around me degraded because I was no longer paying so much attention to them anymore despite the fact that I greatly appreciated their presence in my life. This isn't because they took offense at anything I did or didn't do; in fact, I don't know if anyone has consciously noticed. Rather, I stopped caring about others so much, and distance grew as a natural result of this reduced level of care.
- My physical living space degraded into relative squalor. If I thought about cleaning not as "pending task to be done" (in which case it makes no difference if it's been pending for a day or a week), but instead as "percentage of time that my space is clean by my standards of cleanliness," then my space was almost never clean anymore. Once the high of passion wore off, the squalor made for a constant background drag on my quality of life.
- A sense of learned helplessness resurfaced. The degradation of my physical environment became a constant reminder that I was no longer capable of taking care of my space in the same ways I had before. My sleep schedule fell later and later until it ran up against the hard backstop of the earliest morning meeting I had. When that meeting got moved later in the day, so did my sleep cycle. The fundamental facets of my daily life were defined much more by external forces than by myself.
- A wave of the common cold seems to have rippled through Siem Reap, getting a few people including myself sick at the same time. I rested as much as my mind would let me, but it was becoming clear that my body could not keep up with the pace demanded of it. Proper devotion to the One Thing means sustainable long-term mental *and* physical devotion. As such, bodily health also got subsumed into the monocause of my life. What is good for my physical health is good for the Monocause because the Monocause suffers when physical health suffers, and what is good for the Monocause is good for my health because it gives me the mental confidence to rest properly knowing that the Monocause is in good shape. The Monocause is the manifestation of [the fundamental interconnectedness of all things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirk_Gently) as applied to my life. Notably, I found that the monocausal experience was a lot more enjoyable than the dichotomic separation of life as delineated by a "work-life balance."

And so it was time to pay another visit to [a past state of agency](/blog/v1.2.0/). I had first accidentally entered that state via various catalysts of diverse sizes and origins. I now recognized that familiar feeling of learned helplessness in waiting around for a catalyst to come save me from a pattern I was stuck in. No, I knew better now. I knew that I could make my own catalysts. I knew I could turn things around on a dime if I genuinely wanted to, and I genuinely wanted to feel that intoxicating rush of agency with conviction once more.

Waking up at 5:30 AM again wasn't hard. The first day I slept at 4 AM; running on barely any sleep for the whole day was obviously a non-starter. But sleep-deprivation combined with a couple melatonin gummies had me sleeping by 9 PM that same night. Finally, I could once again experience the joys of the gorgeous Siem Reap sunrises and chilly 21 C morning air! Cambodia is truly a different country in the morning.

This sudden subjective improvement to my sleep cycle presented unexpected new challenges that I had never anticipated before. The interlocking dysfunctionalities that previously governed my life had [happened to collide](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DnBtoOAhba4) in such a way as to provide for a reasonably productive life that has hitherto gotten me to the places I needed to get to. Sure, it didn't feel great to always be waking up sleep-deprived and in a rush to get started, procrastinating for a bit in the afternoon before the panic set in and I started working late, late into the night until there was intolerably little time left for sleep as I fatalistically repeated the cycle yet again the next day. But if you disregarded the subjective experience and looked only at the raw number of hours spent in a day, you could see that this was a highly efficient way to get things done with minimal willpower. There's no friction to getting started right away because I *have* to get started right away, and while the procrastination in-between makes for low quality off-time, the panic and the late hours combined to get a frightful amount of work done. Once I found myself sleeping better -- that's some hours lost already -- and waking up early in the morning with many enjoyable hours to spare, I ended up wasting all of them because I haven't learned the skills to get to things before I absolutely *need* to. I improve one thing in my life, and the rest of it paradoxically all falls apart.

I feel that this is in some way mirrors the challenges society at large has faced in adapting to new circumstances that erode traditional norms. Take for example the traditional importance placed on long-lived relationships that result in lifelong monogamous marriages. It's coming up on a year since my ex and I broke up. In retrospect, I'm glad it ended when it did. I don't know how that girl knew, but she knew the relationship was over. God, we were such kids back then, and we'd both grown to the point where the relationship would've started to hinder rather than facilitate our continued maturation as human beings. It's of course possible that this is only my post hoc rationalization of events, but I find it hard to see the breakup as the tragedy that the traditional perspective would've presented it as.

In fact, I feel now that I had been done a disservice growing up under the ideal of there being one and only one person I should ever truly fall in love with. The thought of getting "The One" wrong or diluting my love for them had me ever paranoid about catching feelings for a relationship that wouldn't end up lasting. In retrospect, I see that I could've enjoyed the little loves in life a lot more, like the little love present in the company of a distant traveler visiting town: not a love strong enough to cause either of us to fling ourselves to the other end of the globe for each other, but a little love that validates our worth despite our mutual insecurities. A little love not to be feared for its potential to ruin the way a moral life is supposed to go for me, but a little love to be appreciated for the momentary human connection it represents.

But what society deems moral depends entirely on the behaviors society needs to reward. Back in the days of tribal warfare, when the main goal was inflicting maximal damage on the enemy by sneaking up on and ambushing them, the moral good of "courage" was taken to be [individual acts of daring](https://acoup.blog/2021/02/05/collections-the-universal-warrior-part-iia-the-many-faces-of-battle/). On the flip side, fleeing from ambushes was the smart thing to do because if you don't save your own hide, how are you going to regroup with your mates later to plot your revenge? But then the rise of agriculture transformed fleeing into the moral deficit of cowardice, as it meant [abandoning the non-mobile assets](https://acoup.blog/2023/03/31/michael-taylor-on-john-keegans-the-face-of-battle-a-retrospective/) your village's livelihood depended on. With the evolution of the hoplite phalanx and the dependence of every man in the phalanx on the others in it, "courage" was redefined to mean standing steadfastly in place even as your brethren died left and right around you. History appears to have changed so slowly that throughout all of this, soldiers by and large never realized just how arbitrary their societal norms around courage and cowardice were.

Far from being an objective moral good, the cultural enforcement of lifelong monogamous marriages also appears to be a byproduct of various societal needs coinciding in monogamy as a preferred solution. The development of agriculture and the subsequent accumulation of wealth that it enabled appears to have [incentivized](https://digitalcommons.kennesaw.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1043) society to take seriously the inheritance of this wealth, and monogamy [naturally emerged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monogamy#Cultural_arguments) as a clean and orderly solution. The social inequalities that arose from wealth accumulation [may have incentivized](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221772273_The_Puzzle_of_Mmonogamous_Marriage) monogamy as a means of tamping down on the societal instability that comes from excessively intense intrasexual competition. As society built upon this system of monogamy to the point where [it could start assuming](https://www.hyrumslaw.com) that all respectable members of society would be participating in it, the costs started rising for everyone who didn't get with the program. In a modern society of socially atomized individuals, single parenthood is tough and makes for worse outcomes for kids. Moreover, the trauma of witnessing a bitter parental divorce can negatively affect a child's attachment style for decades to come, producing knock-on effects well into the next generation. The traditionalists have rightfully pointed out the societal ills of straying from tried and true ways of living.

But while divorce might make for negative outcomes in current society, it intuitively seems that it wouldn't have been so bad in our ancestral environment of a hunter-gatherer camp. It takes a village to raise a child, and single parenthood wouldn't present as much of a challenge in the presence of abundant [allomothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allomothering_in_humans), especially with all the older siblings and cousins that would be present with a higher birthrate. And good luck robbing your ex of their visitation rights once your kid is old enough to wander into another tent of their own accord. As society [entered into an era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_revolution) where defection from the mainstream became much more feasible, we naturally rediscovered [a great variety](https://carta.anthropogeny.org/moca/topics/pair-bonding) of alternate modes of relating to each other, many of which appear to have historically been [informed by environmental factors](https://carta.anthropogeny.org/libraries/bibliography/mating-system-foragers-standard-cross-cultural-sample) such as pathogen load or the importance of male contributions to subsistence. The reformists have rightfully exposed lifelong monogamy for procreative purposes as a moral standard as arbitrary as any other and thrown off the shackles of agricultural shame around casual sex, divorce, and childlessness.

And then come the consequences of undoing societal dysfunctionalities. Cultures that had accreted various other adaptive dysfunctionalities on top of this one suddenly find the whole edifice falling apart as interlocking strands of arbitrary cultural norms become undone and falling birthrates [portend difficulties](https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/dependency-and-depopulation-confronting-the-consequences-of-a-new-demographic-reality) for the developed economies that are hopelessly dependent on continual growth. Yet I don't think the departure from tradition is to blame. The departure was inevitable because the environment that once shaped the traditions had changed to instead prove highly corrosive to those very same traditions, so if blame is to be cast it's to be cast at the uncaring environment instead. Perhaps conditions are ripe for a return to [some traditional forms of meaning-making](https://thecatholicherald.com/article/more-americans-joining-catholic-church-than-leaving-for-first-time-in-decades). But I suspect that for the general population at large, the genie is out of the bottle. We have seen too much to go back to believing in things like the hijab to be unalloyed moral goods, and the only way out is through.

(Given the high personal costs paid to maintain restrictive cultural norms around premarital chastity and the forced continuation of marriages even in the face of severe underlying issues, I think it can be reasonable to model such restrictive cultural norms as adaptive dysfunctionalities. After all, adaptations taken on the societal level aren't necessarily good for the population. Early agriculture resulted in [worse nutritional outcomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neolithic_Revolution#Consequences), including lower life expectancy and shorter stature, even for the elites of those societies as compared to hunter gatherers. Along with the wealth inequalities noted earlier and the [inception of slavery as an institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domar_serfdom_model), one could see why agriculture might be called "[the worst mistake in the history of the human race](https://web.cs.ucdavis.edu/~rogaway/classes/188/materials/Diamond-TheWorstMistakeInTheHistoryOfTheHumanRace.pdf)." Yet agricultural society persisted anyways because they proved more powerful due to their higher population density. The societies that survive, survive, even if their individuals end up worse off than the individuals in societies that didn't survive. It's almost as if there's an [anthropic principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic_principle) for morality, wherein you'll most likely find yourself growing up shaped by morals designed for The System rather than your own healthy personal growth.)

Our societies, cultures, and economies are the way they currently are, operating inside of the geographical, biological, social, political, and technological environments that are the way those currently are. The population suffers so because under such a setup, macro trends resulting in population-scale suffering are a statistical inevitability. It would seem to me that this is a [scale-invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariance) framing that applies equally well for smaller groups of humans as well, as in the case of a recent spat between a friend and her relative. My friend is the particular way she is at this point in time, her relative is the particular way *they* are at this point in time, and their personalities interact inside of the [path dependent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_dependence) context of their prior relationship history. They suffer so because under such a setup, flare-ups resulting in mutual suffering are a statistical inevitability. Even if they understand their own dynamic, understanding alone does not magically undo their mutual triggers. And what applies to them, applies to me as well. My mind and my body are the specific way they are right now, operating inside of the context of my life that is the specific way it is right now. I suffer so because under such a setup, acts of self-sabotage resulting in personal suffering are a statistical inevitability.

I suffer because I must. But inevitability is liberation. The question isn't how I can avoid suffering, because I can't. The question is [what flavor of suffering](/blog/v1.0.0/) I choose to taste. "Not choosing" will not ease the suffering any more than choosing will. Choice is empowering, and so at this point in my life, I choose the empowered flavor of suffering.
